Connect every concept to real defense policy implications. | --- ## Learning Objectives By the end of this module, participants will be able to: 1. **Define** core AI concepts — machine learning, neural networks, and foundation models — in non-technical terms suitable for defense policy decision-making. 2. **Explain** how AI systems are trained and why data and computing power (chips) are strategic assets. 3. **Analyze** the U.S.–China technology competition and its implications for ASEAN defense supply chains. 4. **Connect** global AI infrastructure trends to Cambodia's own digital modernization agenda (Digital Economy Policy 2021–2035 and National AI Strategy 2025–2030). --- ## Duration & Schedule | Time | Segment | |---|---| | 0:00 – 0:15 | Hook Activity: "AI in the Room" | | 0:15 – 1:00 | Direct Instruction Block 1: What Is AI? Post on a visible slide throughout. | Term | Plain-Language Definition | |---|---| | **Artificial Intelligence (AI)** | Computer systems designed to perform tasks that normally require human intelligence — such as recognizing images, translating language, or predicting outcomes. | | **Machine Learning (ML)** | A method of training AI by exposing it to large amounts of data so it learns patterns, rather than following hand-coded rules. | | **Neural Network** | An AI architecture loosely inspired by the human brain — layers of connected processing nodes that learn to recognize complex patterns. | | **Foundation Model** | A very large AI system trained on vast, general datasets (text, images, code) that can then be adapted for many specific tasks. Examples: GPT-4, Gemini, Claude. | | **Semiconductor / Chip** | The microscopic electronic circuits (often made of silicon) that power all computing devices. Advanced chips are essential for training AI systems. | | **GPU (Graphics Processing Unit)** | A type of chip originally designed for video games, now essential for training AI because it processes many calculations simultaneously. | | **Supply Chain** | The network of companies and countries involved in producing and delivering a product — in this case, the hardware and software that makes AI possible. | | **Digital Sovereignty** | A nation's ability to control its own digital infrastructure, data, and technology systems without depending on foreign actors. | --- ## Hook Activity: "AI in the Room" (15 minutes) **Goal:** Activate prior knowledge and generate curiosity before formal instruction. **Instructions:** 1. Instructor shows three images on screen (no labels): - A satellite map with highlighted movement patterns - A smartphone face-unlock screen - A military drone in flight 2. Ask participants: *"Which of these uses Artificial Intelligence? How do you know?"* 3. Take 4–5 quick responses. Do not correct yet — just capture on the board. 4. Reveal: **All three use AI.** Briefly explain how: - Satellite analysis → AI pattern recognition for surveillance - Face unlock → Computer vision / neural networks - Drone → Autonomous navigation and targeting algorithms 5. Transition statement: *"AI is no longer a future technology — it is already embedded in the systems your adversaries and your allies are using today. This module gives you the foundation to understand how it works, where it comes from, and what it means for Cambodia's security."* --- ## Direct Instruction Block 1: What Is AI? Core Concepts (45 minutes) ### Part A: From Rules to Learning (10 min) - Traditional computers follow explicit rules written by programmers. - AI systems **learn from data** — they are trained, not programmed, to recognize patterns. - Analogy: Teaching a soldier to recognize enemy uniforms by showing 10,000 photographs is analogous to training an image-recognition AI. **Key point for defense leaders:** The quality and quantity of training data is a strategic asset — whoever controls the best data has the most capable AI. ### Part B: Machine Learning — How AI Gets Smarter (15 min) Explain the three main types of machine learning using military analogies: | ML Type | How It Works | Military Analogy | |---|---|---| | **Supervised Learning** | Train on labeled examples (input + correct answer) | Training a recruit with answered drills — here is the question, here is the correct response | | **Unsupervised Learning** | Find patterns in unlabeled data | Intelligence analysts reviewing intercepted signals without knowing what to look for | | **Reinforcement Learning** | AI learns by trial and error, rewarded for good outcomes | War-gaming simulations where the AI plays thousands of scenarios and learns winning strategies | ### Part C: Foundation Models — The New Power Centers (20 min) - A foundation model is trained once on enormous datasets (trillions of words, images, code). - It can then be fine-tuned for specific tasks: medical diagnosis, legal analysis, military intelligence, translation. - Key examples relevant to defense: - **GPT-4 / ChatGPT** (OpenAI, U.S.) — language understanding and generation - **Gemini** (Google, U.S.) — multimodal (text + images + video) - **ERNIE Bot / Wenxin** (Baidu, China) — Chinese-language dominant - **Claude** (Anthropic, U.S.) — strong at long-document analysis and reasoning - **Hunyuan** (Tencent, China) — enterprise and government applications **Strategic point:** Control over which foundation model a country's military uses determines whose data governance rules, whose servers, and whose potential backdoors are in play. --- ## Guided Practice: Concept Mapping Exercise (20 minutes) **Goal:** Solidify the AI vocabulary and concepts before moving to the geopolitical layer. **Instructions (pairs or trios):** 1. Each pair receives a blank concept map template with "AI" at the center. 2. They must connect and label at least 6 of the 8 vocabulary terms using arrows and relationship phrases (e.g., "AI uses → Machine Learning which requires → Data"). 3. Groups share their maps with a neighboring pair and discuss differences. **Debrief question:** *"Which of these concepts do you think is most relevant to Cambodia's current defense capability — and why?"* --- ## Direct Instruction Block 2: Digital Supply Chains & Geopolitical Competition (40 minutes) ### Part A: Why Chips Are the New Oil (15 min) - Modern AI requires enormous computational power — specifically, advanced semiconductor chips (GPUs, TPUs). - The global supply chain for advanced chips is highly concentrated: - **Design:** Primarily U.S. companies (NVIDIA, AMD, Qualcomm) and ARM (UK/Japan) - **Manufacturing:** ~90% of the world's most advanced chips are made by **TSMC** (Taiwan) - **Equipment:** Netherlands' ASML makes the lithography machines that no country can replace - **The strategic vulnerability:** If TSMC is disrupted (conflict over Taiwan), global AI development halts — including military AI programs worldwide. **Cambodia implication:** Cambodia imports nearly all its digital hardware. Any major disruption in the semiconductor supply chain directly affects the country's ability to modernize its defense digital infrastructure. ### Part B: U.S.–China Technology Competition (15 min) Key flashpoints for strategic context: | Event | Significance | |---|---| | U.S. chip export controls (2022–2024) | U.S. bans sale of advanced NVIDIA chips (A100, H100) to China — forcing China to develop domestic alternatives | | China's "New Generation AI Development Plan" | China's state-directed roadmap to lead global AI by 2030 | | Huawei Ascend chips | China's attempt to build domestically competitive AI chips without U.S. technology | | U.S. CHIPS Act (2022) | $52 billion U.S. investment to re-shore semiconductor manufacturing | | ASEAN caught in the middle | ASEAN nations face pressure to align with one technology ecosystem — risking exclusion from the other | **Key message:** This is not merely a trade dispute — it is a contest over who controls the foundational infrastructure of military AI globally. ### Part C: Implications for ASEAN and Cambodia (10 min) - ASEAN nations, including Cambodia, are primary consumers of AI technology — not yet producers. - The risk: **Strategic dependency** — relying on one power's AI infrastructure (hardware, software, cloud) for defense creates intelligence and sovereignty vulnerabilities. - Cambodia's response: National AI Strategy 2025–2030, Tier 4 national data center, Cloud-First Policy, and digital government modernization. **Discussion prompt:** *"If Cambodia's military adopts an AI system built on U.S. cloud infrastructure — and tomorrow that access is restricted as it was for some allies — what is the contingency plan?"* --- ## Group Analysis Exercise: The Chip War and Cambodia (45 minutes) **Goal:** Apply geopolitical supply chain concepts to a Cambodia-specific strategic scenario. **Setup:** Groups of 4–5 participants. Each group receives the printed case study: *"TSMC, NVIDIA, and the Semiconductor Supply Chain."* **Task:** Each group answers three questions and prepares a 3-minute verbal brief: 1. **Vulnerability Map:** Identify three points in the global chip supply chain where a disruption would most impact Cambodia's digital defense modernization plans. 2. **Strategic Options:** What steps could Cambodia take to reduce its dependency on any single nation's technology ecosystem while maintaining modernization pace? 3. **ASEAN Opportunity:** How could ASEAN-level cooperation (referencing the ADMM AI Joint Statement, February 2025) create collective resilience against supply chain disruptions? **Debrief:** Instructor draws out common themes, highlights divergent perspectives, and connects group findings to Module 3 (Cambodia's national legal and policy framework). --- ## Exit Assessment (20 minutes) **Format:** Individual written response on exit ticket card (or A4 paper). Participants answer TWO of the following three questions (their choice): 1. In 3–4 sentences, explain what a "foundation model" is and why access to one is a strategic issue for a nation's defense ministry. 2. Describe one way the U.S.–China technology competition could affect Cambodia's ability to procure AI-enabled defense systems in the next five years. 3. What is one concrete step Cambodia should take to improve its "digital sovereignty" in the defense sector? Justify your answer. **Instructor use:** Review exit tickets before Module 2 to identify gaps and tailor the opening review accordingly. --- ## Differentiation Notes | Participant Profile | Adaptation | |---|---| | **High technical background** (IT/signals officers) | Provide supplementary reading: NVIDIA GPU architecture brief; invite them to co-explain a technical concept during instruction | | **Policy/legal background** (defense lawyers, planners) | Emphasize governance and treaty implications; direct toward Module 4 preview | | **Senior commanders** (limited time for detail) | Ensure all key concepts are summarized in the one-pager glossary; focus discussion on strategic implications, not mechanics | | **Participants from non-Khmer backgrounds** | Provide translated glossary (Khmer/English) and ensure case study materials are available in both languages | --- ## Assessment Alignment | Objective | Assessment Method | |---|---| | Define core AI concepts | Concept Mapping Exercise (Guided Practice) | | Explain AI training and chip dependency | Exit Ticket Question 1 | | Analyze U.S.–China competition | Group Analysis brief + Exit Ticket Question 2 | | Connect to Cambodia's policy context | Exit Ticket Question 3 + Group Analysis Question 3 | --- ## Instructor Notes - **Avoid deep technical jargon.** The goal is strategic literacy, not engineering competence.
